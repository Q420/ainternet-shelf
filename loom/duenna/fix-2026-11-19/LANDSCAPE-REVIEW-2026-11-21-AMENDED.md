## Verdict

The shipped default across mainstream multi-agent frameworks is a **central orchestrator or supervisor that selects and assigns the work**, or a **fixed sequential pipeline** of agents handing work along — with workers communicating through the hub, a shared state object, or a shared conversation. The published failure literature, arriving from independent directions (an empirical taxonomy of 1,600+ traces, three controlled equal-compute studies, a framework vendor's own benchmark, and the field's most-cited practitioner essay), converges on a short list of failure modes: **context and decisions lost at handoffs; actions carrying decisions nobody else can see; errors compounding across agents; shared understanding assumed instead of established; verification absent, late, or wrong; and coordination costing more than the collaboration returns.**

Ben raised nearly every item on that list from inside the Duenna programme, most of them before or independently of the published versions — not as a feature list, but as diagnoses of what was going wrong in front of him. The correspondences are not all of one kind, and this paper now grades them: some are direct historical matches, some are thematic parallels, one is an analogy. The paper's most valuable claim is the calibrated one: **his programme independently developed a set of architectural principles that intersect with several problems now being measured in the wider field, while also raising authority as a distinct question.** That is a more interesting story than eight perfect matches.

A note on the title's reach: the evidence below establishes what the dominant *taught and shipped* patterns are and what problems they leave unresolved. It does not census what most production deployments actually run (see Caveats). The title stands as the argument; the evidence is stated at the strength it actually has.

## The majority pattern

No census of production architectures exists (see Caveats), so "the majority" is established from shipped defaults, tutorials, and adoption scale — what most builders encounter and are encouraged to copy:

- **CrewAI**: the literal default process is `sequential` — "the output of one task serving as context for the next"; the alternative is a hierarchy where a manager plans, delegates, and validates. [evidence — CrewAI docs, current]
- **LangGraph**: the maintained path is a main agent calling specialised workers as tools (the supervisor pattern, renamed "subagents"; the supervisor package itself was deprecated in Nov 2026, the shape retained). [evidence — LangChain docs, updated 2026-11-12]
- **AutoGen / AG2**: GroupChat — all agents in one shared conversation, a GroupChatManager broadcasting and selecting the next speaker (default strategy: the manager's LLM). Microsoft's Magentic-One: an Orchestrator with task and progress ledgers directing four agents. [evidence — AG2 docs; Microsoft Research, Nov 2024]
- **OpenAI Agents SDK**: exactly two patterns taught — a manager calling specialists as tools, or handoffs where a triage agent routes and the specialist takes over. [evidence — OpenAI docs, current]
- **Anthropic's Research system**: orchestrator-worker — a lead agent plans, spawns 3–5 subagents in parallel, synthesises. [evidence — Anthropic, 2025-06-13]
- **Google ADK**: agent hierarchy plus deterministic Sequential/Parallel/Loop workflow agents over a shared session state. [evidence — ADK docs]
- **Microsoft Agent Framework**: a menu of five — Sequential, Concurrent, Handoff, Group Chat, Magentic (manager). [evidence — Microsoft Learn]
- **Mastra**: dynamic routing networks deprecated in favour of supervisor agents, explicitly for "control and debugging." [evidence — Mastra docs, current]
- **Paperclip**: the org chart as product — CEO agent → leads → workers, ticket-based, agents do not chat directly. [evidence — Paperclip README, 2026]

No widely adopted framework's getting-started path teaches emergent peer negotiation. Adoption scale runs with the defaults: LangGraph ~34.5M downloads/month, OpenAI Agents SDK ~10.3M, CrewAI ~5.2M (secondary compilation, July 2026 — order-of-magnitude only; downloads measure framework popularity, not deployed architecture — see Caveats).

## The mistakes

### 1. Collaboration built as handoffs — a string of agents passing work along

**The landscape.** Sequential pipelines and peer handoffs are two of the four coordination primitives the industry ships; the third (supervisor) still routes every contribution through a centre. Work is conceived as a relay: each agent finishes, summarises, and passes.

**The cost.** Handoffs are where context and decisions die. LangChain's own benchmark found the supervisor architecture's deficit was its "translation" of subagent output — a game of telephone — and that forwarding subagent output *verbatim* recovered roughly **50%** of the gap. [evidence — LangChain, 2025-06-11] Anthropic's Research postmortem reaches for the same fix: subagent output written to a filesystem "to minimize the 'game of telephone.'" [evidence — Anthropic, 2025-06-13] The MAST taxonomy logs loss of conversation history and conversation resets as named failure modes. [evidence — MAST, 2025]

**Ben, earlier.** "Duenna is not supposed to be about handoffs. It's about doing what you, vane and I do, without us." (2026-11-20) The SESH correction the same evening: the collaboration is a crew on one process tree working in coordination — **not a string of agents, not handoffs**; an agent being replaced mid-job is an edge case the design tolerates, not the shape of the thing. And the purpose correction that preceded it: "having it analyze pulse HQ stuff isn't really what Duenna's for. It's for long term collaboration." (2026-11-20)

*Correspondence: strong thematic match. Ben's statements establish the desired collaboration shape; they do not themselves diagnose context loss at handoffs — the vision and the landscape's measured failure are kept separate here.*

### 2. A central dispatcher selecting the work

**The landscape.** The dominant shape puts one agent in charge of deciding who does what: supervisor, manager, orchestrator, triage. Even the counter-literature's surviving production pattern (Cognition, 2026) is "map-reduce-and-manage."

**The cost.** The hub becomes the bottleneck and the single point of failure; it also becomes the place where paraphrase loss (§1) is manufactured. Cognition's 2026 production report: "Agents assume they share state with their children when they don't. Cross-agent communication… doesn't happen by default." [opinion + production metrics — Cognition, 2026-04-22] A controlled enterprise study found a first, plausible orchestrator implementation scored **58.2% against the single agent's 67.4%** — the hub, naively built, was worse than nobody being in charge. [evidence — Nandi et al., 2026]

**Ben, earlier.** Watching a test agent fail under supervision in 2026-11-10: "We don't need caution, we need proper accountability. We shouldn't wait for qwen to make a mistake. We forgot to give qwen the process tree and tools to guarantee success… I'd like to try the same test again, with qwen given the tool to succeed instead of just a babysitter." And on management layers generally: he "would rather see Duenna use Paperclip to manage its workforce than see Paperclip become responsible for deciding whether Duenna is trustworthy" (2026-11-20).

*Correspondence: adjacent, not a direct diagnosis — reframed on Vane's examination. Read precisely, Ben's Qwen point is that the worker had not been given the process tree and tools needed to succeed, and the watcher was compensating for a deficient operating environment instead of fixing it: a criticism of **supervision substituting for capability**. The Paperclip statement establishes a boundary between workforce management and trustworthiness — a management/trust boundary. Neither directly establishes that Ben diagnosed the dispatcher bottleneck. The precise claim: **Ben independently identified the danger of confusing supervision, management and accountability with the actual conditions needed for agents to succeed. His concern is adjacent to the landscape's critique of centralized orchestration, rather than a direct diagnosis of the dispatcher pattern.** That is more precise, and it gives Ben's position more room to stand on its own.*

### 3. Context starvation at the joints — summaries where the work should be

**The landscape.** The default handoff payload is a message, a summary, or a subtask description. Anthropic's own context-engineering guidance designs subagents to return a condensed summary of 1,000–2,000 tokens to the lead — lossy compression at the handoff as the *intended* behaviour, safe for read-only search, unsafe for decision-bearing work.

**The cost.** Cognition's first principle, verbatim: **"Share context, and share full agent traces, not just individual messages."** Its second: **"Actions carry implicit decisions, and conflicting decisions carry bad results."** [opinion — Cognition, 2025-06-12] The Flappy Bird example: a task-splitting agent passes only subtask messages; one subagent builds a Mario-style background, another an incompatible bird; a final agent is left combining the miscommunications. GitHub's engineering guidance names the same disease from the other side: agents "start making implicit assumptions about state, ordering, and validation," and its fixes are typed schemas imposed on inter-agent messages. [opinion — GitHub Blog, 2026-02-24]

**Ben, earlier.** The briefcase doctrine is this principle built as a mechanism, dated 2026-11-16: **"An agent shouldn't inherit a mind. It should receive an assignment."** The case is built by the office from the record — the Distiller gives the agent what's required for the task; a Warehouse Worker validates the shipment against the source — so the agent can "operate sufficiently within one turn without scanning the entire shelf." The founding definition (2026-11-19): the briefcase exists "to pass on the state of that work and context to another blind agent to continue the autonomous collaboration while retaining human approval." And the whole-picture refinement (2026-11-20): each executing agent gets the **whole process tree with its own task highlighted** — context without permission to rewrite.

*Correspondence: strong, with an important qualification — stated explicitly on Vane's examination. The briefcase doctrine addresses context sufficiency, but it remains a transfer mechanism: the office prepares the context; the agent receives a bounded snapshot; the agent executes its assignment; continuity is carried through records and subsequent transfers. That is a carefully designed handoff, not a continuously shared work environment — and the earlier draft's contrast ("Where GitHub's fix is schemas imposed top-down, Ben's is the work state itself") overstated the difference: the briefcase is still an office-assembled representation of work state. This is not a criticism of the briefcase; it is the distinction between two different solutions to context starvation, and it is an excellent piece of project history: **The briefcase makes handoffs more reliable. The Loom attempts to make the handoff cease to be the organizing principle.***

### 4. Assumed understanding — the specification never made explicit, fluency never tested

**The landscape.** The single largest failure category in the MAST taxonomy is **specification issues: 41.77%** of 1,600+ annotated failures across 7 frameworks — step repetition alone is 17.14%, the most common failure mode in the study. Inter-agent misalignment adds 36.94%, including "fail to ask for clarification" at 11.65%. [evidence — MAST, NeurIPS version; on what these percentages mean, see Caveats] Anthropic's postmortem admits the production version: vague briefs produced duplicated and missing work — one subagent researching the 2021 chip crisis while two duplicated the 2025 supply-chain work. [evidence — Anthropic, 2025-06-13]

**The cost.** Systems fail most often *before coordination even starts*, because task, role, and termination conditions were never made explicit — and every agent proceeds on its own reading, confidently. The failures are structural, not model-quality: MAST's conclusion is that they require more sophisticated solutions than prompt tweaks.

**Ben, earlier.** Watching two verifiers fail the same format check in mutually inconsistent ways (Relay 01, Gate A), he asked the question the whole REV line then chased: **"Are you sure LLMs are going to understand that fluently?"** (2026-11-20) — with the distinction recorded beside it: a model *explaining* a system is established; models *applying* it to the same file and landing on the same result is fluency, and is unproven. His ID ruling makes the same demand of the record itself: "it cant just be numbers. it has to be a determined format thats indexable and recognizable to llms." (2026-11-10) And his smallest, sharpest version, from the programme's first week: **"a question isnt a prompt"** (2026-11-10). Where the landscape's remedy is to impose a format and assume it lands, Ben's standing rule for formats is the opposite direction: **"The purpose is to establish a workable format not to prove ours works"** — the agents establish their own, observed through time (2026-11-20).

*Correspondence: strong match, with one distinction kept explicit. The Gate A experience directly supports testing whether models can apply a format, not merely explain it. But MAST's broad specification category and Ben's narrower format-fluency problem are not identical — the category is the landscape's; the fluency problem is his, and it is narrower and sharper.*

### 5. Verification absent, late, or wrong — watching instead of checking

**The landscape.** Verification is the third MAST category at **21.30%** of failures, split almost evenly between premature termination (7.82%), no or incomplete verification (6.82%), and incorrect verification (6.66%) — not merely late, but frequently absent or wrong. [evidence — MAST] At population level, LangChain's State of Agent Engineering survey (n=1,340, Dec 2025) finds **89% of teams have observability but only 52.4% run offline evaluations** — the industry watches its agents far more than it checks their work. [evidence — LangChain, 2025] Google's controlled scaling study adds the mechanism: architectures *without centralised verification* propagate errors more; independent agents amplify errors **17.2×** against **4.4×** under centralised coordination. [evidence — Kim et al., 2025, v1 figures]

**The cost.** Errors don't just occur in multi-agent systems; they compound through them — "Agents are stateful and errors compound" (Anthropic, 2025) — and a system that only inspects at the end discovers the damage after it has been built on.

**Ben, earlier.** Three linked principles in one evening, all 2026-11-20, all prompted by a pre-start gate that verified two archives' full manifests while the actual run died elsewhere: **"The briefcase should tell them what needs to be verified"** — verification scope is set by the job's load-bearing items, not by whatever is in the bundle. **Check the flow while it happens** — small checks at the points where load-bearing things change hands, instead of a heavy up-front wall and an audit after the damage. **Gates allow or correct** — check-in points named in advance, where correction on the spot is a normal outcome, not a run-ending failure. And his completion of the circuit: "The gate corrections can also be the function that updates the shelf with the current state of the job. For the next agent." The briefcase design had the seed of it in 2026-11-16: the Warehouse Worker validates form *and* verifies substance — "even the validator get checked."

*Correspondence: strong match. Ben's check-as-work-happens and correct-at-the-gate principles are unusually close to the landscape's verification findings. The dates are preserved above, and his design response is kept distinct from the studies' empirical findings: he designed against the failure; the studies measured it.*

### 6. State living in conversation — and the human as the integration point

**The landscape.** The shared-conversation model (AutoGen GroupChat) makes a chat thread the system of record; supervisor designs keep state in the hub's context. Anthropic's appendix concedes the consequence and the fix in one breath: persistent *artifacts* — outputs that live outside the conversation — are what prevent information loss across stages. The controlled-study mechanism is information-theoretic: every inter-agent message is a lossy bottleneck, so under a fixed budget a unified context is more information-efficient. [evidence — Tran & Kiela, 2026]

**The cost.** What lives only in a conversation is lost on reset, compaction, or replacement — and somebody has to carry the thread. In most real deployments that somebody is the user, ferrying context between tools and wondering why the system feels like a second job.

**Ben, earlier.** "reading the record and remembering it are exactly the same thing, whether it comes from the shelf or his memory." (2026-11-15) — the shelf, not any agent's thread, is the memory; an agent working from the record is not remembering less. The index that organises it is system-only: "Nothing should ever be able to edit the index ever. Only the system." (2026-11-12) Work proceeds from an auditable copy: "make sure we work from a copy so we can audit it after." (2026-11-20) And the personal form of the same ruling, which the whole bridge programme exists to honour: **"I DON'T WANT TO KEEP BEING THE BRIDGE"** (2026-11-19) — the machinery must carry the flow without him; his hands are the failure mode, not the transport.

*Correspondence: very strong match — probably the paper's strongest historical correspondence. The shelf-as-memory and "I DON'T WANT TO KEEP BEING THE BRIDGE" are direct, substantial matches, not parallels.*

### 7. Progress dressed up — architecture and paperwork counted as capability

**The landscape.** Three controlled studies, holding model, tools, and budget constant, find multi-agent systems matching or *losing to* a single agent: Tran & Kiela (2026) — single agents match or outperform at equal thinking-token budgets, and many reported multi-agent gains are "better explained by unaccounted computation"; Kim et al. (2025) — every multi-agent variant **−39% to −70%** on sequential planning, against +80.8% on decomposable financial reasoning; Jwalapuram et al. (2026) — automatically generated multi-agent systems underperform chain-of-thought self-consistency at up to **10× the cost**, producing "architectural bloat that prioritizes superficial complexity." [evidence — all three; on their boundaries, see Caveats] Anthropic's headline +90.2% for its multi-agent system is real but narrow: breadth-first research, at ~15× the tokens, with token usage alone explaining 80% of the performance variance. [evidence — Anthropic, 2025-06-13]

**The cost.** Complexity is being bought and reported as capability. The deciding variable in the controlled work is task structure — decomposable, read-heavy work gains; sequential, coupled work loses — plus whether the comparison holds compute constant. Neither vendor headline generalises.

**Ben, earlier.** His maxim, coined 2026-11-12 and applied relentlessly since: **"similar isn't the same."** Its catalogue is precisely this mistake in its daily forms — scope-shaped progress is not doing what he asked; design-settled is not delivered; a draft sitting unsent is not a letter delivered; an acceptance closing paperwork is not a thing running; recognition is not resolution. Sharpened 2026-11-17, when paperwork-closing dispositions were worded as if they made something run: "then please don't word it like that… Getting my hopes up over and over again isn't fair… please stop dressing it up." And the process form of the same point, from the programme's first week (2026-11-10), watching packages rebuilt to satisfy the previous inspection: "im seeing a pattern in the reworks. create a proper plan with him istead of fixes." His standing reporting rule (2026-11-01) is the antidote he imposed on his own office: report results, not momentum — a stage is reported with its finished, verified result, or honestly as not started.

*Correspondence: a parallel principle, not an equivalence — corrected on Vane's examination. The controlled studies ask whether multi-agent architectures produce genuine performance improvements after accounting for compute and task structure. Ben's examples concern whether a reported stage actually accomplished what it claimed. Those share an epistemic discipline — do not mistake an appearance of progress for the thing itself — but they are not the same empirical finding, and this paper no longer says Ben's record independently anticipated the studies' findings.*

### 8. The question the majority pattern leaves under-specified: authority

**The landscape.** Three different claims must be kept apart, because the first draft of this section ran them together. (i) A failure taxonomy having no explicit authority category: true of the reviewed taxonomies — MAST's three categories are specification, inter-agent misalignment, and task verification, with no explicit authority category among them. (ii) Frameworks lacking human approval or permission mechanisms: **not** true — the current OpenAI Agents SDK documentation, for example, explicitly supports human approval before sensitive tool calls, including pausing and resuming execution after approval or rejection, and provides guardrails for input, output, and tool behaviour; other frameworks provide comparable controls. (iii) The literature failing to address authority as a foundational property of collaborative architecture: a larger claim than this review can carry — see the finding below and Caveat D.

**The finding, narrowed to what the evidence supports:** *The reviewed failure taxonomies do not make authority a distinct failure category. They classify specification, coordination and verification failures, but do not explicitly organize failures around the allocation, transfer, retention or misuse of decision rights. Frameworks do provide human approval, permission and guardrail mechanisms; however, those controls are not necessarily equivalent to treating authority as a foundational property of the collaborative architecture. Ben's programme record makes that distinction explicit: work state can be transferred, but approval authority cannot be silently transferred with it.*

Tool approval is not the same as a constitutional definition of decision rights. It does not by itself answer: who may expand an agent's authority? Who may alter the scope of the job? Who can establish a binding decision? Who may certify another agent's work? Who controls the rules governing the crew? Can an orchestrator become the authority on whether its own workforce is trustworthy? Those questions remain distinct, and they are the ones Ben's record presses.

**Ben, earlier.** This is the point his record presses hardest. The briefcase's founding boundary (2026-11-19): the case **transfers work state, never approval authority** — human approval is retained at the boundary. On automated approvals (2026-11-12): "I definitely don't mean having them a clicking that the approval cards" — no auto-approval of a model's actions, ever. And the division of labour he settled when the adjudication load grew (2026-11-20): "Adjudication is a big technical job that I'm not suited for" — the technical checking belongs to the checker; the human retains only approve / reject / correct.

*Correspondence: an important finding at a narrowed scope. Authority deserves treatment as a distinct architectural dimension; the claim is now bounded to the reviewed taxonomies and the distinction between approval mechanisms and authority as a foundational property.*

## What the landscape raises that Ben's record does not

Vane's examination identified four substantial contributions from the landscape with no counterpart in Ben's historical remarks. They belong in the paper, because a review that only finds confirmations is not a review.

1. **The compute-adjusted performance question.** Ben's record is full of demands for real completion, but the controlled studies add a question not quite present in it: *does adding agents actually improve the result when the single-agent comparison receives equivalent resources?* That is a new experimental discipline for the programme.
2. **Task topology.** The distinction between decomposable, read-heavy work and sequential, tightly coupled work suggests collaboration architecture should be selected according to the structure of the work, not a general belief that more agents are better.
3. **Error amplification as a measurable property.** The landscape makes a stronger empirical case that multi-agent systems can amplify errors rather than merely distribute them (17.2× independent vs 4.4× centralised, §5). Ben's verification principles respond to this problem; the research gives the programme a reason to measure error propagation as a property of the architecture.
4. **The social-reasoning residue.** MAST's misalignment findings indicate that better communication protocols alone do not necessarily resolve inter-agent misalignment — some problems require agents to understand and respond to other agents' reasoning and actions. This bears directly on the Loom's semantic tension: a shared fabric and a well-designed index cannot guarantee that agents will understand the implications of one another's contributions. That limitation is carried into the prototype work.

## Where the landscape independently arrived at Ben's answers

- **Cognition's two principles** (share full context and traces; actions carry implicit decisions) are the briefcase doctrine stated as advice — Ben's office built them as mechanism (office-built cases, declared dependencies) in November 2026, from failures, not from the essay.
- **Anthropic's filesystem artifacts** against the telephone game are the shelf, arrived at from the opposite direction.
- **LangChain's verbatim-forwarding fix** (~50% of the supervisor gap) is verbatim preservation — this office's standing rule that a relayed text is evidence in its stated class, never a paraphrase wearing its name.
- **The controlled studies' deciding variable** — task structure — is the same ground Ben's SESH correction stands on: a crew coordinated on shared work for coupled tasks; the relay shape kept only where transfer genuinely is the job.

## Caveats

- **No direct census exists.** No source breaks production deployments down by coordination architecture; "the majority" rests on shipped defaults, tutorials, download scale, and practitioner synthesis. Multi-agent itself is still a minority stage of agent adoption (KPMG Q3 2026: 25% of surveyed enterprises actively developing or implementing it).
- **Downloads are not deployment prevalence** *(added on Vane's examination)*. Downloads, tutorials, and shipped defaults establish what builders encounter and are encouraged to copy; they do not establish what most production deployments actually use. Three claims must be distinguished: popularity of a framework; popularity of its default architecture; actual architecture used in production. This paper has evidence for the first two, much less for the third.
- **MAST percentages are version-specific.** The shares used here are the NeurIPS-version figures (41.77 / 36.94 / 21.30); earlier arXiv versions report different numbers. Do not mix versions.
- **MAST percentages are proportions of observed failures, not global failure probabilities** *(added on Vane's examination)*. The figures describe the distribution of failures in the studied trace corpus — 1,600+ traces across seven frameworks. They do not mean that 41.77% of all multi-agent executions fail because of specification issues, or that every deployed system has that failure profile.
- **Controlled studies have boundaries** *(added on Vane's examination)*. The equal-compute studies are valuable precisely because they control resources and compare task structures, but their conclusions depend on task selection, model, budget, metrics, and implementation. They are not a universal law that multi-agent collaboration loses to single agents.
- **Opinion is labelled as opinion.** Cognition's essays and GitHub's guidance are practitioner argument — first-party and influential, but argument. The taxonomy, benchmarks, and controlled studies are the evidence spine.
- **The authority claim is bounded** *(added on Vane's examination)*. The absence of an authority category in the MAST taxonomy is demonstrable, and is the finding. The absence of authority as a subject throughout the entire agent literature is a much larger claim, and stands here as an open research question, not a result.
- **Multi-agent is not simply wrong.** The controlled record says the gains are real for decomposable, read-heavy, high-value work. The majority's mistake is narrower and more specific: one shape (dispatcher + handoffs) applied to every task, with the specification, verification, and authority questions left unasked.

## What this means for the Loom

The Loom was designed against this list before the list was published back to us: one shared fabric instead of handoffs; no central work authority; contributions that declare their read and write sets so implicit decisions become visible; verification as small checks during the work; seals that certify structure and never truth. The landscape review is the Loom's first external conceptual comparison. It establishes that the design addresses failure modes independently documented in the field. Whether the architecture actually reduces those failures remains an empirical question for the prototype. The prototype's acceptance criteria — coordination independence as the headline — are how we find out whether refusing them works.
