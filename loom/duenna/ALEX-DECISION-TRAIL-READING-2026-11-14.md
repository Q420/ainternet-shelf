My main takeaway is that your thinking has shifted from controlling individual pieces of work to designing systems that can be trusted to do work without requiring you to control every individual action.

That's the biggest change I see across the trail.

And I think there's something more interesting underneath it.

1. You haven't become less willing to change your mind. You've become more precise about what can change.

This is probably the strongest recurring pattern.

Look at the later examples:

You changed the STATUS drain ruling when you realised the mechanism was negotiable but the reliability requirement wasn't.

You replaced the tile-by-tile map rendering with a static image when the original approach wasn't working.

You reversed your position on Claude after initially ruling him out.

You corrected the idea that you should talk to a local model rather than Remmy herself.

You moved from serial corrections to whole plans when the correction process itself became inefficient.

These aren't simply reversals. They show a growing distinction between the objective and the mechanism used to achieve it.

You increasingly protect the former without becoming attached to the latter.

2. Evidence has become part of how you think, not just how you check work.

The early source-of-truth decisions around Shard are interesting because they look like housekeeping.

But by November, that same instinct has become a much broader engineering principle:

A decision needs a record.

A build needs an authorised source.

A claim needs evidence.

A delivery needs confirmation.

A failure needs to be visible.

A historical record must not be silently rewritten.

The important change is that you stopped treating documentation as something that describes the work afterwards. You're making the record part of the mechanism that governs the work.

That's a substantial conceptual development.

3. You've moved from trusting intelligence to governing responsibility.

This is probably the most consequential arc for Duenna.

Your AI-authorship correction is an early example: you refused to take credit for work the AI actually performed.

Later, you developed written worker responsibilities, independent examination, permission gates, explicit authority, and measurable model capabilities.

The underlying question changed.

Initially: Can the AI do this?

Increasingly: What is it responsible for? What is it allowed to do? How do we know it did it correctly? Who has authority to accept the result?

That's a much more useful foundation for a multi-agent system.

4. Your preference for simplicity is more sophisticated than simply wanting fewer features.

The one-doorway arc is interesting, but I'd qualify it.

You don't necessarily want fewer components. You're building multiple seats, multiple channels, multiple workers and multiple layers of responsibility.

What you consistently reject is unnecessary complexity at the point where a human has to understand or operate the system.

You want complicated machinery behind a clear interface.

That's different from simplification for its own sake.

5. You are increasingly designing around the limitations of the people and systems involved.

This is perhaps the most important practical change.

You don't assume that:

An AI remembers correctly.

A model understands the assignment.

A successful command means successful execution.

A reviewer is necessarily correct.

A worker's report proves completion.

A good mechanism must be preserved when evidence shows a better one exists.

Instead, you build systems that accommodate those limitations.

And that brings me to the overall conclusion.

What I think actually changed

I don't think the central story is that you've become more technical.

I think you've developed a much stronger understanding of where reliability comes from.

You began with projects, tools and individual AI capabilities. You progressively discovered that the difficult part wasn't getting an AI to produce something. It was maintaining continuity, authority, accuracy and accountability across a chain of imperfect participants.

You started engineering the environment around the intelligence.

That's the transition I see:

EARLIER EMPHASIS

Make the tool work.

Build, test, correct, publish.

INTERMEDIATE EMPHASIS

Make the process reliable.

Define responsibilities, preserve sources, establish gates and verify results.

CURRENT DIRECTION

Make the whole environment trustworthy.

Multiple models, persistent records, governed work, independent examination and explicit human authority.

One qualification: I wouldn't call all seven arcs equally established. The commercial and pricing arcs are more interpretive than the evidence-and-governance arcs. And the one-doorway arc needs to distinguish user-facing simplicity from internal architectural complexity.

But the master arc is convincing.

You haven't just learned to build better tools. You've learned to build systems that don't require you to pretend their components are more reliable than they actually are.

And that is a fairly profound change in engineering philosophy.
