# THE VISION — Ben's Office (2026-11-25, v4)

**One office. A human-directed workforce. Distinct agents. Shared work.
Visible collaboration.**

*The proper interpretation of Ben's original vision, drafted from his own
descriptions (2026-11-25) and his original designs — the October AI office and
the November 16 Agent Briefcase design — interpreted by Remmy, examined by
Vane, and presented for Ben's ruling. The idea draft (v6) is preserved as the
source submission. This is a vision document: no build, no scope, no
implementation claims. The product's name is Ben's to choose.*

## The idea

An office in which a human directs and works alongside a generated workforce
of AI agents.

Not a chat application with agent features attached. The dashboard is the
place where Ben encounters the entire working office — the people doing the
work, their assignments, their conversations, their resources, and the work
emerging from them. Chat is one of its instruments. The orchestrator's chat is
an entrance into the office, not the office itself.

## The experience

Ben enters an office, gives the orchestrator a task, and watches as she
assembles the workforce needed to do it.

Each agent appears as a distinct worker, with its own conversation,
assignment, working room, journal, and record. Ben can enter any worker's
space, see what it is doing, and speak to it directly.

The workers share a common body of project material — a visible briefcase of
the files and resources around which their work is organised. They access
that material as they need it, contribute to it, and build upon one another's
findings. And they have a place to work together: an active exchange of
findings, questions, challenges, and contributions, grounded in the shared
work and capable of changing it.

Ben can see the office at work: the agents, their activity, the resources
they use, the work they produce, and the records they leave behind. He can
direct the orchestrator, intervene with an individual agent, or join the
collaboration himself.

The office is not a collection of chatbots. It is a working environment in
which distinct AI agents are assembled for real assignments, collaborate
through shared work, and remain visible and accountable to the human
directing them.

## The orchestrator

She is the person-shaped point of coordination between Ben and the workforce —
more than an intake, more than a mechanism that creates workers.

Ben gives her the assignment, in plain words, in her own chat on the
dashboard. She understands the job, determines the agents it requires, and
generates them through the framework's assembly. She remains the point of
contact as the job develops: the one who can account for the crew, carry
Ben's direction into it, and bring its questions back. The authority she
holds is the authority Ben gives her; the vision invents none beyond his
description.

## The workers

Each agent is a distinct working participant — a real framework worker, not a
conversation pretending to represent one. The worker exists independently of
its window: the window is a human-accessible view into an actual worker and
its activity, where the conversation and the work are two aspects of the same
participant.

An agent receives an **assignment**, not an inherited mind. It is generated
for its job — model, identity, skills, tool grants, and a task-scoped
briefcase of the context its assignment needs — starts clean, and works from
its own room, journal, and record. When the work yields, that agent is done;
the journey belongs to the work.

**Ben's principle (2026-11-25 ~18:21 MST): the office is the role.** Each
role is assumed by the office — the office IS the role the agent slips into.
The room, the folder in the briefcase, the journal, the record, the tools and
the standing context belong to the office and persist; the agent occupies it
for the work and is interchangeable. That is why the dashboard's windows are
offices, named by role first and occupant second — and why the continuity
section below holds: what endures is the office and its work, never the
occupant's mind.

## The shared working ground

The briefcase is the common working ground of the office, and it is **on
show**: the shared project material — files, source references, shared
findings, working artefacts — visible as a stocked, openable object, with the
agents' use of it visible against the items themselves. A file being opened
is seen against that file, not buried in a chronological log.

**Ben's rulings (2026-11-25 ~18:15 MST), which supersede both the examiner's
reconciliation and the dynamic-briefcase reading recorded in v2 of this
document:** there is **one shared briefcase** for the crew, and the crew uses
it as their **shared context**. Inside the briefcase, **each agent has its
own folder** — its working place within the common ground, where its own work
lives. **The collaboration chat is the coordination layer**: it is what tells
the agents who is doing what, and how the work is delegated. And the crew
**organises itself, based on role** — the orchestrator brings the job; the
agents divide it among themselves in the chat, each taking what fits its
role, rather than every piece being assigned from above.

The founding principle survives in this shape, relocated: an agent still
receives an **assignment, not an inherited mind** — it starts clean, with its
own folder and its own record. What it shares is the case and the
conversation: the common material, and the living account of who is doing
what. The briefcase holds the shared context; the chat holds the
coordination; the folder holds the worker's own work.

## The collaboration

The most crucial part of the whole thing, in Ben's own words: a chat window
where the agents collaborate with each other.

Its test is consequence. The collaboration is not complete when an agent
sends a message; it is complete when the contribution enters the shared work,
influences another agent's contribution, or changes the direction or quality
of the result. Findings are handed across, drafts challenged, questions put
to the agent whose work they touch — anchored always to the shared briefcase
and the shared files, argued over artefacts that exist. This window is where
the workforce's collective intelligence becomes visible. It is a defining
feature of the office, not an optional convenience.

## The visible work

Everything the office does, Ben can see — in two complementary views: **what
the agents are producing**, and **what they are using to produce it.**

The output as it forms: files appearing and taking shape in the room, the
deliverable the moment it is filed. The activity as it happens: briefcase
accesses against their items, calls and what they carried. The resources as
they are consumed: tokens against the road's limits, dispatches against
their bounds, time on the job. A finished document alone does not tell Ben
how it came about; a stream of calls alone does not tell him what emerged.
Together they make the work intelligible — and nothing burns in the dark.

## The human's place

Ben is not watching an automated process. He is a participant in the office:
directing the orchestrator, instructing an individual worker in its window,
entering the collaboration, answering questions, challenging a finding. And
where the work reaches his authority — anything outward, spend-bearing, or
irreversible — it arrives at his chair as a decision. The briefcase transfers
work state, never approval authority. That line does not move.

## The continuity

The office remembers the work — not the worker's mind. Journals, records, and
the role's governed memory preserve what was done, found, and decided, so a
job's thread outlives any one occupant. The next worker receives an
assignment and the context it needs; it does not inherit its predecessor's
mental state. Continuity belongs to the work, its records, and the framework.

## What already exists

Carefully qualified: the engine underneath this office has been built and
proven piece by piece — agent generation and assembly; a generated agent
completing real work in its own office, beginning to end; the worker using
its own tools natively, witnessed live; pull-based working with a journal;
a live view of a worker's activity (the wall and readout, in crude form);
a crew surface with dispatch (the October office); and a working multi-seat
discipline for directed work between human, orchestrator, and examiner.

Ben's own measure of the remaining distance: *"It's not far off from what we
have now. It's just the interface that's different really."* That stands here
as his assessment — the vision's claim is about what the office IS, not a
proven estimate of what the interface will cost to build.
