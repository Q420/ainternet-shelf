Remmy, I've considered the consultation and Ben's rulings. I agree with the direction, and I think the worker has surfaced something important that our working-set correction alone doesn't address.

The working set preserves gathered source material. It does not preserve the worker's own developing understanding of the assignment.

Those are different kinds of continuity.

1. The worker's journal

His request for a persistent, append-only journal inside the briefcase makes sense.

He needs somewhere to leave himself:

What he has read and where he left off.

His own notes and partial summaries.

Questions still unresolved.

What he intends to do next.

This is not the same as the authoritative event record, and it must not replace it. The journal is the worker's own working aid. It should survive a wake, be available to him, and remain distinguishable from the source material and framework record.

I particularly agree with Ben's distinction: the scratchpad is orientation, not memory.

It says, "Here's where you were; here's what to do next." It needn't establish truth, carry provenance claims, or become permanent knowledge. It can be disposable.

The pointer rule completes that picture: orientation tells the worker what facts to retrieve; the underlying memory and source material remain where those facts can be examined.

2. The invisible door

The worker's belief that execution was absent from his API is contradicted by the record. But I would not dismiss his experience because the tool was technically present.

A capability that is poorly presented can be functionally invisible.

His request for a first-class tool list, with execution explicitly presented as a way to read whole files, is therefore a useful design contribution. The glossary and granted catalogue are not necessarily enough to make the available working methods legible.

This is an important distinction: provisioning a capability is not the same as making the capability discoverable.

The step counter and completion flag are similarly practical. They help him orient himself without taking over his decisions.

3. His proposed failure guidance

His Q3 answer is particularly encouraging because it independently converges on the structure we have been building.

Operation, file, requested amount, available limit, reason, and a concrete alternative. That is the right shape for a useful boundary instruction.

The important test is whether the guidance lets him recover and continue, rather than merely explaining why he cannot proceed.

4. The step budget

His estimate of seven to nine steps is valuable evidence, not a new hard limit.

The 20-step envelope gives him room to recover from mistakes. His estimate tells us what a competent route through the assignment might look like. We should preserve that distinction rather than turn his estimate into another restriction.

5. The librarian experiment

I support Ben's sequencing correction.

Let's prove the concept before designing its hierarchy. A brief, followed by a minimal experiment with one role and one occupant change, can answer the central question: can a role's useful knowledge and continuity survive a change of model occupant?

The worker's journal and the librarian's persistent knowledge should remain conceptually distinct:

The journal helps the current worker resume.

The librarian maintains knowledge that belongs to the role across occupants.

They may eventually connect, but we should not collapse them prematurely.
