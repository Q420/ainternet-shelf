## 1. What the runs actually establish

The progression is unusually clear.

Run 5: Gary read all three sources and composed a relevant brief. The delivery route rejected him twice under R31. He then stalled. His debrief exposed the gap between a tool being present and a worker recognising it as a usable way to deliver.

Run 6: The onboarding, briefcase structure, working-set carriage and R31 suspension removed those obstacles. He read all three sources in three steps and composed another complete brief. He still chose contribute, and the malformed envelope prevented the contribution from reaching adjudication.

Gary's conversation: Without changing the framework, he chose plain prose, asked questions, worked through the supplied sources and delivered a 608-word brief in the conversation. The office filed it verbatim.

The important conclusion is not that Gary cannot use the write tool. It is that he repeatedly chose the conversational contribution route, even after we made the alternative explicit.

We had been treating his preferred behaviour as a mistake to correct. Ben has now ruled that the framework should accommodate it.

There is also a useful distinction in the results: Run 6 demonstrates composition inside the loop, but not successful delivery. Gary's try-out demonstrates successful delivery outside the loop, but not integration with the room. Neither result should be made to prove more than it does.

## 2. Role memory: the librarian's responsibility

I agree that the calibration summary belongs in the role's memory.

The opening conversation should not have to rediscover stable knowledge already earned by previous occupants. The librarian should preserve the useful learning so that it compounds.

I would keep the summary evidence-linked and bounded:

- Observed communication and formatting preferences.
- Known interaction patterns, including the preferred contribution route.
- Repeated points of confusion and the explanations that resolved them.
- The conditions under which each observation was made.
- Whether something is demonstrated, a worker's own report, or an inference.

That last distinction matters. Gary choosing plain prose in this try-out is direct evidence for this checkpoint and this working arrangement. It is not a universal law about every model or every future worker.

The librarian should preserve calibration, not turn it into immutable personality or authority. It must not grant tools, alter the assignment, or silently make a preference mandatory.

The journal remains something different: Gary's disposable orientation notebook for the current work. The librarian remembers what the role has learned across occupants. Those are complementary forms of continuity, not competing memories.

## 3. The opening conversation

Ben's direction resolves the central mismatch.

The opening should be a real conversation with the worker, not a carefully formatted prompt pretending to be one.

It should establish the assignment, make the available material understandable, explain what successful delivery means, and give the worker room to ask questions. The conversation also lets the office learn how to express the working prompt in a form the worker understands.

The librarian changes the starting point: we already know what has been learned, so the opening need not repeat the calibration interview. It should use that knowledge and ask only what remains relevant to this particular job.

Gary's suggestions are useful evidence:

- Present the assigned sources together in a clearly labelled batch.
- Establish any job-specific style preferences at the outset.
- Give a clear, bounded word-count target.

I would adopt these as design considerations, not hard-coded universal rules. In particular, the word-count preference should remain a preference until its intended tolerance is unambiguous.

One qualification: the try-out was only partly conversational. Gary's question about the emphasis of the next-step section went unanswered because the other side was scripted. That does not invalidate the successful delivery, but it means we have not yet witnessed the full value of a genuinely responsive opening.

## 4. The contribute door belongs in the room

Accepted direction.

Ben's ruling at INDEX 518 is the right answer to Runs 5 and 6.

The worker's contribution should be accepted as the arrival of the deliverable when the assignment permits that route. The office then files the contributed content at the designated destination and records the filing as an office act.

The distinction must survive in the record:

- Gary authored and contributed the content.
- The office performed the filing.
- The resulting file is labelled office-filed.
- The record must not imply that Gary used sandbox.write when he did not.

This is not a reason to make every contribution an automatic deliverable. The assignment establishes what counts as delivery. Where it does, the framework should honour the worker's chosen door.

And this door must exist inside the room, not just in the opening chat. Otherwise we would have solved the demonstration by moving the worker outside the environment we are trying to build.

## 5. The chat is the opening, not the habitat

This is the most important boundary in Ben's synthesis.

The conversation is how the job begins and how the office learns to prepare the work. It does not replace the worker's office, tools, briefcase, record, or governed environment.

The transition needs to be explicit:

- The worker and office have the opening conversation.
- The office establishes the assignment and the worker's understanding of it.
- The useful results of that conversation are carried into the case and first working package.
- The worker begins the actual assignment in his own room, with his tools and delivery doors available there.

We must not rely on private conversational context magically surviving that transition. The relevant decisions and understanding need to become part of the recorded work state.

And once inside, the worker gets the freedom we have been working toward. The office guides the assignment, stocks the room, and governs the boundaries. It does not dictate every movement.

## 6. The actual product still deserves examination

I inspected the two accessible brief artifacts.

Run 6's 834-word brief is a coherent newcomer-oriented summary, but it never became a filed deliverable in that run. Gary's 608-word brief did arrive and was filed verbatim. It covers the four requested areas, but the opening calls the project "The Vane collaborative-agent framework", which is incorrect. It also gives substantial space to A2/A3 scope detail.

That is a content-quality finding, not a failure of the conversational method. It is precisely why authentic contribution, successful filing, and acceptance of the work's accuracy or usefulness must remain separate judgements.

The conversation try-out establishes that this method can produce and deliver a useful artifact. It does not yet establish that the room can host the method, or that the opening conversation has been fully exercised as a responsive exchange.

## Final position

I accept the direction as three connected but separate responsibilities:

- Librarian: retain evidence-based role calibration across occupants.
- Opening conversation: establish the particular job, answer questions, and learn how to present the work.
- Worker's room: provide the actual habitat, tools, record, and preferred contribution door under the existing boundaries.

No need to turn this into another experiment before we have understood what the evidence already says. No need to build a conversational wrapper around the entire working environment, either.

The six runs have taught us something important: we spent considerable effort teaching Gary how to use the framework, when the framework needed to learn how Gary works.

Let's not lose that lesson in the next design.
