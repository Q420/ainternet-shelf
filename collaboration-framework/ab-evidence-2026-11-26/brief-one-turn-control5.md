The framework that the office calls Vane is a controlled environment for a standing job that runs in a single turn. It is built around persistent seats that keep a bounded record of all activity through a doorway, bubble, and office system. The core of the framework is the Stage 2 accountability foundation, which includes the record, doorway, bubble, seat drivers, office, onboarding, gates, orchestrator, observer and checker. These components have been implemented, their behaviour recorded, and their refusal paths defined. The checker is structurally separated from the rest of the machinery, and its independence is an architectural property that has been tested, but it does not guarantee that the checker’s model of the world is correct.

A1, A2 and A3 are mechanisms that have passed examination. A1 proves that a generated worker instance can be assembled, its grants sealed, and a bounded loop can be started with record perception, tool requests, temporal limits and outcome accounting. A2 proves that tool grants are enforced from the sealed assembly record, that dispatches are fingerprint‑bound, that the executor identity is distinct, that each dispatch is counted and that reconciliation is performed only by the examiner on uncertain dispatches. A3 proves that the loop can repeatedly present a case view, receive a response, validate it, and adjudicate the act, that bounds are pinned to the exact case version, that the deadline is derived from a monotonic activation anchor, that stalls are recorded only after dispatch accounting closes, and that terminal exclusivity is enforced. All three pieces have been examined, corrected and accepted, and the implementation scope for each is now closed.

What remains unproven is the complete founding proposition that a generated worker, created for a particular task, receives only its authorized resources, perceives the shared work, makes useful progress, records its actions, yields in a condition from which another independently generated worker can continue, and can be transferred between agents without the authority, context or accountability moving accidentally. This proposition has not been demonstrated with generated workers. A4, A5 and A6 are intended to close essential parts of it, but even after A6 the blind‑handoff demonstration only shows that continuity machinery works; it does not prove that a real job can be completed. The runtime can make the execution bounded and attributable, but it cannot make the underlying model competent. The piece‑by‑piece passing of A1–A3 hides integration risk at the seams, and our strongest evidence comes from simulations where the checker, test suite and examiner all operate on constructed evidence. Outside evidence such as a live‑record replay of a generated worker doing a real bounded assignment, or an independent assessment of the quality of the work product, is still missing. Moreover, recorded termination of an execution, revocation of a credential, or an uncertain outcome are not proof that the remote operation ceased, that every copy of a credential vanished, or that the side effect has been resolved.

The honest next step, as recommended by Vane, is to pause before moving into A4. First we must agree on what the existing evidence actually establishes, and then we should build and examine A4, A5 and A6 to prove the missing pieces. The next concrete action is to run a live‑record replay of a generated worker completing a real bounded assignment, to demonstrate that the worker can perceive the shared environment, make progress, record its work, yield, and allow a blind successor to continue. Only after that demonstration can we claim that the framework supports genuine agent collaboration.
