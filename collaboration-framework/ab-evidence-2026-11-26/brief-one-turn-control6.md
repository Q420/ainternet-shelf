In the November 23, 2026 stock‑take that Ben requested, Vane and Remmy stepped back to review the current state of the project. The effort is organized around a single‑turn control that tries to make a standing job complete in one turn. The architecture is built on a Stage 2 accountability foundation that records every action, defines a doorway that gates entry, creates bubbles that isolate the worker, and uses seat drivers, an office, onboarding, gates, an orchestrator, an observer and a checker to enforce rules. The checker is architecturally independent, but its independence is an architectural property that has been tested; it does not guarantee that the checker’s model of the world is correct.

Three layers of achievement have been demonstrated. First, A1 provides a mechanism for assembling a worker instance against a specific task and identity, sealing the grants into that instance and enforcing them at dispatch. Second, A2 supplies a mechanism for granting tool access, fingerprinting each request, deduplicating retries, counting dispatches against a bound, and recording each dispatch with a distinct executor identity. The executor claims each dispatch durably, records a start event from inside the executing thread, re‑checks the deadline before performing work, and accounts every outcome, including uncertainty. Third, A3 delivers a bounded loop that repeatedly presents the case view, the record delta, settled tool results, and standing dispatch states; it validates whole responses, adjudicates each act and request through the doorway, and records stalls only after dispatch accounting closes. The loop’s bounds are pinned to the exact case version that existed at activation, the deadline is derived from a monotonic activation anchor, and the loop cannot begin after the deadline has expired. All three mechanisms have passed their respective examinations and their implementation scopes are closed.

What remains unproven is the full proposition that a generated worker can be created for a real, bounded assignment, perceive the shared work, make useful progress, record its actions, and then yield a state in which another independently generated worker can continue without inheriting the first worker’s private context. A4, A5 and A6 are intended to close essential parts of that proposition: A4 would govern access to the shared brain and skills and control the publication of durable work; A5 would establish a real boundary between a worker’s active authority and the continuing work, including yield, termination and continuation; A6 would demonstrate a blind handoff that proves continuity belongs to the work rather than to the outgoing agent’s private state. None of these have been shown, and the adapter’s evidence chain is still open until a live‑record replay is performed. Moreover, the founding‑picture test — whether work can move between independently generated agents without authority, context or accountability moving by accident — has not been demonstrated with generated workers.

Four worries are highlighted. First, a correct execution mechanism does not make the worker competent; the runtime can bound and attribute behaviour but cannot make the underlying model capable of reasoning well, selecting useful actions, recovering from poor results or producing quality work. Second, passing each piece individually can hide integration risk at the seams; a component may be locally correct while the overall system fails when the pieces are combined under a single assignment. Third, the strongest evidence currently comes from environments that are easy to simulate — the checker, test suites and examiner all operate on constructed evidence. Outside evidence such as a live‑record replay of the adapter, a real generated worker completing a genuine job, and an independent assessment of the work product are needed as counterweights. Fourth, boundaries versus substrate must be proved: a recorded termination does not prove that a remote model operation ceased, a revoked credential does not prove that every copy of that credential vanished, and an uncertain outcome does not prove that an external side effect has been resolved.

The honest next step, as recommended by Vane, is to pause before moving into A4. The team should first agree on exactly what the existing evidence establishes: that the accountability foundation is real, that A1–A3 are implemented mechanisms, and that the loop in A3 can be executed within defined bounds. Only after that consensus is reached should they decide what new evidence is required to prove the remaining parts of the founding proposition. Rather than spending another month building machinery that assumes a generated worker can complete a modest real assignment, they should test a first generated worker on a modest task and discover early whether it can succeed. That failure would provide useful learning that a clean test suite cannot, and it would clarify whether the framework can support genuine agent collaboration.
