The framework that the office calls Vane is a control system that attempts to manage an entire standing job in a single turn by using a collection of mechanisms that record, bound, and account for the actions of generated worker instances. It is built on a Stage 2 accountability foundation that includes a record, a doorway, a bubble, seat drivers, an office, onboarding, gates, an orchestrator, an observer and a checker. The checker is architecturally independent, but its independence is an architectural property that has been tested, not a guarantee that its model of the world is correct.

What is proven:

- A1 is a passed mechanism that defines how a worker instance is assembled against a specific task and identity declaration, how its grants are sealed into the assembly record and enforced at dispatch, and how the instance is created under the framework’s rules.  
- A2 is a passed mechanism that shows how tool requests are mediated, how dispatches are recorded, how the grant source is the sealed assembly record, how request identity is deduplicated, how bounds are counted in dispatches, and how per‑tool registry checkers enforce ceiling constraints. The A2 implementation has passed a suite of 76 checks, all regressions are green at the exact baseline, and the scope is confined to A2.  
- A3 is a passed mechanism that defines a bounded loop in which each iteration presents the case view, the record delta, settled tool results (including uncertainty), standing dispatch states and the bounds state; it validates whole‑response shape, adjudicates acts and tool requests, and records stalls only after dispatch accounting closes. The A3 implementation has passed a suite of 77 checks, all regressions are green, and the scope is confined to A3.  
- The Stage 2 foundation is real and has been implemented; it provides persistent seats, a bounded record‑and‑doorway system, and a structural separation between the framework and the checker. The checker’s independence is an architectural property that has been verified, but it does not guarantee that the checker’s view of the world is accurate.

What remains unproven:

- The complete founding proposition – that a generated worker can be created for a particular bounded assignment, perceive only its authorized resources, make useful progress, record its actions, yield in a state where another independently generated worker can continue, and that the work can move between such workers without the accidental transfer of authority, context or accountability – has not been demonstrated.  
- A4, A5 and A6, which close essential parts of that proposition, are not yet present. A4 would govern access to the shared brain and skills and control the publication of durable work; A5 would establish a real boundary between a worker’s active authority and the continuing work, including yield, termination and continuation; A6 would provide a blind‑handoff demonstration that continuity belongs to the work rather than to the outgoing agent’s private context. None of these have been shown.  
- The adapter’s evidence chain is still open because the required live‑record replay has not been performed. Until that replay is done, the adapter’s full evidence cannot be considered closed.  
- The ability of work to move between independently generated agents without the accidental movement of authority, context or accountability has not been demonstrated with generated workers.  
- Even if A6 passes, a successful handoff demonstration does not prove that a real job can be completed; it only proves that the continuity machinery works, not that the machinery can support useful work.

Four worries are highlighted in the sources:

1. A correct execution mechanism does not make the model competent. The runtime can bound, attribute and account actions, but it cannot ensure that the worker selects useful actions, recovers from poor results or produces quality work.  
2. Passing each piece separately hides integration risk at the seams; a component may be locally correct while the overall system fails when the pieces are combined under a single assignment.  
3. The strongest evidence comes from simulated components (the checker, test suites and examiner) while outside evidence such as a live‑record replay or a real generated worker doing a real job is the needed counterweight.  
4. Boundaries versus the underlying substrate are uncertain; a recorded termination is not proof that a remote model operation ceased, revocation of a credential does not guarantee that every copy vanished, and an uncertain outcome does not resolve an external side effect.

The honest next step, as recommended by Vane, is to pause and agree on what the current evidence actually establishes before proceeding to A4. The priority is to test whether the first generated worker can complete a modest real bounded assignment. If that test fails, it will reveal a fundamental limitation early, rather than spending additional time building machinery that assumes the worker can succeed. The concrete next step is therefore to design and run an end‑to‑end experiment in which a generated worker is created, given only its authorized resources, performs a bounded task, records its progress, yields, and allows a blind successor to continue. Until such a test is performed, the framework remains a set of proven mechanisms but not a proven agent workforce.
