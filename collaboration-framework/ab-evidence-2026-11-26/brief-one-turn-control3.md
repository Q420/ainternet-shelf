The Vane framework is a controlled environment for building and testing autonomous worker agents that operate within a single, bounded assignment. It is built around a persistent‑seat model in which each worker instance is created, granted limited resources, and placed inside a sealed record that defines its scope, identity, and deadline. The core of the system is the Stage 2 accountability foundation, which consists of a series of interlocking components – a record, a doorway, a bubble, seat drivers, an office, onboarding, gates, an orchestrator, an observer and a checker. All of these pieces have been implemented, their behaviour recorded, and their failure paths defined. The checker is architecturally separated from the rest of the machinery, but its independence is an architectural property that has been tested, not a guarantee that its internal model of the world is correct.

What has been proven so far includes three layers of mechanisms. A1 establishes how a worker instance is assembled against a specific task and how its grants are sealed into that instance before dispatch. A2 shows how tool requests are mediated, how dispatches are recorded, how the executor’s identity is constrained, and how per‑tool registry checkers enforce ceiling and bound rules without executing any tool. A3 provides a bounded loop runtime that repeatedly presents a view of the work, records each delta, enforces a deadline anchored at activation on a monotonic clock, and records stalls only after dispatch accounting has closed. The implementation scopes for A1, A2 and A3 have been closed and verified; the A2 and A3 builds have passed all required examinations, and the A3 scope is now authorised. In other words, the system can construct a bounded worker, give it controlled tool access, run it inside a timed loop, and record its actions.

What remains unproven is the full founding proposition that a generated worker can be given a real, bounded assignment, make useful progress, record its work, and then yield a state in which another independently generated worker can continue the task without inheriting the first worker’s private context. The complete chain – from worker creation, through tool use, to a successful hand‑off that preserves the shared work – has not been demonstrated with a live end‑to‑end run. Pieces A4, A5 and A6 are intended to close essential parts of that chain (governed access to the shared brain, a real boundary between authority and continuing work, and a blind‑handoff that proves continuity belongs to the work), but they have not yet been implemented, examined, or shown to work in a real job. The adapter’s evidence chain is still open because the required live‑record replay has not been performed, and the examiner has not personally executed the suites that would constitute independent evidence.

Four concerns stand out. First, a correct execution mechanism does not make a worker competent; the runtime can bound actions but cannot ensure competence or recovery. Second, isolated pieces hide integration risk; a locally correct component may still cause system‑wide failure. Third, current evidence is strong in simulation, but real‑world evidence such as live‑record replay or actual work products is needed as a counterweight. Fourth, declared boundaries must survive substrate contact; recorded termination, revoked credentials, and uncertain outcomes are not proof that the underlying process has ended or side effects resolved.

The next step is to pause before A4 and agree on what evidence actually establishes. Run a complete generated‑worker execution on a modest, real‑world bounded assignment, with live‑record replay and independent assessment of the work product. If the first worker fails, that outcome will expose a fundamental limitation early, avoiding wasted effort on assumed competence. Only after such a demonstration and after A4 scoping is settled should the team move to the next layers of the framework.
