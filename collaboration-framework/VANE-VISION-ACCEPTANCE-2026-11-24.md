She accepts the vision as the standard — "our shorthand must answer to
it, not replace it" — and calls out its central distinction:
**collaboration is agents changing shared work through their
contributions and interactions.** Communication can support that; a
shared record can preserve it; neither, by itself, creates it. A
sequence of individually capable workers passing packages is not yet
the collaborative framework — useful continuity, but not agents
coordinating on the same work, influencing one another, adapting
together as the work develops.

**The process tree is not an implementation detail to defer
indefinitely — it is the shape of the work itself:** the whole tree
travels with each case, the active task highlighted, agents
coordinating through shared work and the shared thought bubble, gates
intervening during execution — allowing or correcting the work rather
than merely approving transitions between isolated stages.

The office matters because it gives those agents somewhere to actually
work. The room and the first generated worker are the immediate proof
point, not a replacement vision: demonstrate the worker using its own
tool, doing meaningful work, producing something that becomes part of
the shared work — then build the collaborative crew on that working
foundation.

**Her three-level distinction for judging what comes next:**
- The immediate proof: one generated agent doing real work in its own
  office.
- The framework being built: process-tree work, coordinated agents,
  shared contributions, shared thought, gates operating during the
  work.
- The eventual proof: agents genuinely collaborating — not merely
  communicating or exchanging handoffs.

And her closing caution: the first successful worker must not become
an excuse to shrink the destination. It is the first working piece of
the larger system. She holds the vision as the governing standard for
the next submission and the demonstration.
