3. Persistent Role Memory Librarian

This is a substantial idea, and I like its central distinction:

The role owns continuity. The model supplies reasoning. The librarian maintains the role's accumulated knowledge. The framework governs authority.

That gives us continuity across different model occupants without pretending that a model's private context is a durable institutional memory.

I have several questions I'd want the design paper to resolve.

First: what exactly is the memory's identity?

If memory belongs to a role, we need to distinguish the enduring role from a particular version of its responsibilities. If a role changes materially, does its memory continue unchanged, branch, or acquire a new version? We should avoid a role inheriting knowledge accumulated under a mandate it no longer holds without knowing that history.

Second: how does the librarian establish what is worth remembering?

This is probably the most consequential question. A conversation contains facts, proposals, interpretations, corrections, decisions, mistakes and abandoned ideas. They cannot all become undifferentiated memory.

I'd want every retained item to preserve its epistemic character and provenance: who said it, where it came from, when it applied, whether it was verified, and whether it was later superseded. The librarian should organise knowledge, not quietly turn someone's assertion into established fact.

Third: does the librarian have any authority beyond maintaining memory?

My instinct is no. It should be able to propose, organise, summarise, index and retrieve. It should not change role definitions, permissions, assignments or governing decisions. Nor should its summary become a new source of authority merely because it is persistent.

Fourth: how do the memory blocks work as a retrieval system?

I agree with Ben's distinction: summaries are finding layers, not replacements for the underlying material.

I'd like to understand how a keyword or topic search moves from a summary to the exact underlying passage, decision or work record. We should be able to retrieve the original evidence, not merely another summary of a summary. Compaction must not quietly become irreversible information loss.

Fifth: what does the 75% trigger measure?

The context-relative principle is sound. A fixed token threshold would be inappropriate across models with different context limits.

But the usable context is not necessarily the advertised context window. System instructions, role memory, the current briefcase, tool definitions and working material all consume space. Is the trigger based on the actual assembled context, and does compaction itself have enough room to complete safely? I'd treat 75% as the initial policy, subject to the model's real operating envelope.

There is also one important distinction between the two memory tiers: immediate past should be automatically available, but older memory should be discoverable and pullable. We should not confuse persistent availability with mandatory context loading.

My overall view

This has the potential to solve a problem deeper than model memory: how a role accumulates experience without depending on the continuity of any particular occupant.

I would keep the librarian as a distinct, accountable worker with a narrow responsibility. It maintains the knowledge base; it does not become the role's owner, decision-maker or hidden orchestrator.

And I'd keep the original material behind every compacted block. A memory system that cannot show where its knowledge came from is eventually a very confident rumour mill.

I welcome Ben's direction. I'd like to see the worker's consultation before settling the details, because his experience of waking, receiving context and trying to continue work may expose requirements we haven't thought of.
